Q1
Update on General Medicine
A 60-year-old female presents with recurrent scleritis. She has no significant past medical history other than some chronic joint inflammation of the hands and feet and ulnar deviation of her fingers. What laboratory test would most likely reveal the underlying cause of her scleritis?
Correct answer: B. rheumatoid factor
Rationale
This patient likely has rheumatoid arthritis. Serologic testing is important in the workup and diagnosis of RA. Rheumatoid factor (RF) is present in 70%–80% of patients. Anti-nuclear antibodies are elevated in many inflammatory diseases. Antiphospholipid antibodies are seen in lupus and other rheumatic diseases. Anti-SS-A antibody tests, along with tests for anti-SS-B antibodies, are used in the detection of Sjögren syndrome.


Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the most common rheumatic disorder, affecting approximately 1% of adults worldwide, and is 3 times more likely to occur in ­women than in men. RA is classically a progressive, symmetric, and deforming peripheral polyarthritis characterized by synovial inflammation and hypertrophy as well as autoantibody production. Although it can involve any joint, RA primarily affects the small joints of the hands and feet. Typically, affected joints are swollen and tender, with decreased range of motion and deformity from bone and cartilage destruction. Hand deformities (Fig 9-1) include nodules, ulnar deviation, Boutonnière deformity (abnormally flexed proximal interphalangeal [PIP] joint and extended distal interphalangeal [DIP] joint), and swan-­neck deformity (abnormally hyperextended PIP and flexed DIP). Early diagnosis and treatment are critical in controlling the joint damage and associated disability.

Extra-­articular Manifestations
Extra-­articular involvement, which is an indicator of disease severity, occurs in about 40% of RA patients over the course of the illness. Risk ­factors for systemic involvement include the presence of rheumatoid ­factor (RF), anti–­cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies, and smoking. RA can affect almost all systems; manifestations include
  • subcutaneous rheumatoid nodules (in approximately 25% of patients)
  • anemia of chronic disease (common in RA patients)
  • increased risk of lymphoma
  • Felty syndrome (seropositive RA, neutropenia, and splenomegaly)
  • osteopenia and a higher risk of fractures
  • pleural effusions, pulmonary nodules, and interstitial fibrosis
  • cardiac disease (coronary artery disease, pericarditis and effusions, valvular disease from rheumatoid nodules, and cardiomyopathy from secondary amyloid deposition)
  • peripheral vascular disease and vasculitis (small-­ to medium-­sized vessels)
  • carpal tunnel syndrome (from synovitis, compressive myelopathy, or radiculopathy)
  • muscle weakness (primary or drug-­induced myopathy)
Ocular involvement may include dry eye disease, scleritis, episcleritis, and corneal inflammation, melting, and infection. The ocular manifestations of RA are discussed in BCSC Section 8, External Disease and Cornea, and Section 9, Uveitis and Ocular Inflammation.

Laboratory Testing
Serologic testing is impor­tant in the workup and diagnosis of RA. Rheumatoid ­factor (RF) is pres­ent in 70%–80% of patients. However, it has limited specificity for the disease, as it can be positive in up to 10% of unaffected individuals and in about one-­third of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Anti-­CCP antibody is as sensitive as RF but has a higher specificity for RA (98%). Nevertheless, both of ­these tests may be negative in up to 50% of RA patients. Similarly, anti–­mutated citrullinated vimentin (anti-­MCV) antibody testing shows high specificity for the disease. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-­reactive protein (CRP) levels are usually elevated. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing is nonspecific, ­because only 30% of RA patients have positive results, but it can help exclude other diseases such as SLE. Although ge­ne­tic testing is not performed routinely, the HLA-­DRB1 gene appears to be the strongest known ge­ne­tic risk ­factor for the development of RA.

Treatment
Treatment of RA involves an integrated approach, incorporating medi­cations and nondrug therapies. Nonpharmacologic interventions include dietary counseling, exercise, physical therapy, smoking cessation, lipid control (to reduce the associated cardiovascular risks), and immunizations (to reduce the risk of infection linked with the use of immunosuppressive agents).
The ultimate goal of pharmacologic treatment (discussed in detail at the end of this chapter) is disease remission. Early diagnosis and treatment are imperative in preventing or delaying the long-­term effects of disease progression. Mild symptoms of joint stiffness and pain can be treated with analgesics and nonsteroidal anti-­inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), although ­these agents do not alter the long-­term prognosis. Glucocorticoids are sometimes helpful in controlling the acute stages of inflammation; severe cases may require sustained low-­dose therapy (<10 mg/day).
Disease-­modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), which are divided into nonbiologic and biologic agents, have been shown to slow disease progression, reduce potential joint destruction, and maintain joint function while also limiting the need for long-­term ste­roid use. In addition to their anti-­inflammatory effect, ­these drugs reduce the body’s heightened autoimmune reaction. Thus, the clinician should consider screening for pre­existing infectious conditions such as hepatitis B and C and tuberculosis. Among nonbiologic agents, methotrexate is generally the first-­line treatment, but other options are available. Biologic agents, developed through ge­ne­tic engineering, are modified proteins that can target cytokines. The largest group of currently available agents consists of anti–­tumor necrosis ­factor (TNF)-­a inhibitors. Other biologics act by blocking interleukin (IL)-1 or IL-6 receptors or in modifying T-­cell or B-­cell activity. One emerging group of DMARDs, which are technically small-­molecule drugs as opposed to true biologic agents, targets the inhibition of the Janus kinase (JAK) enzyme involved in mediating inflammation.

What medication is associated with blue-yellow color defects?
Correct answer: C. digoxin
Rationale
Digoxin is associated with disturbances of color vision, usually blue-yellow pattern defects. The resulting experience can be described as a yellow, green, blue, or red tinge to objects. Some patients also experience glare phenomena, yellow or green flickering vision, or formed visual hallucinations.
Some patients who use amiodarone report hazy vision and colored halos around lights. Corneal whorl-like deposition is common. Amiodarone optic neuropathy can also cause slow progressive bilateral vision loss.
Lisinopril can cause orbital angioedema. Metoprolol can cause keratoconjunctivitis sicca-like syndrome, likely due to decreased lacrimation.

Ophthalmic considerations
Many of the adult patients seen and treated by ophthalmologists are in the age group at risk for IHD and its many complications. They often undergo stressful eye surgery ­under local or general anesthesia, and ophthalmologists need to be cognizant of ­these patients’ risks of myo­car­dial ischemia, myo­car­dial infarction, CHF, and arrhythmias. Similarly, ophthalmologists need to be aware of the association between proliferative diabetic retinopathy and IHD. This information should be given to the primary medical care provider of patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy but no diagnosis of IHD so that appropriate screening tests can be considered.
Cardiac complications of noncardiac surgery are a major cause of perioperative morbidity and mortality, MI being the most significant. Older
age, preexisting CHD, and CHF are the principal risk ­factors for development
of ­these complications.
ICDs are increasingly used for the management of cardiac arrhythmias. Although no cases of discharge of an ICD during ocular surgery have been reported, the ophthalmologist should discuss the status and pos­si­ble perioperative disabling of the ICD with the cardiologist or anesthesiologist before ocular surgery to avoid surgical complications.
Fi­nally, ophthalmologists should be aware of the potential ocular adverse effects associated with medi­cations commonly used in treating CVD. Following are the medi­cations of clinical relevance:
  • Amiodarone. Corneal microdeposits occur in nearly all patients who use amiodarone for an extended period of time (years). The corneal epithelial whorl-­like deposition is indistinguishable from that due to chloroquine. Visual changes are unusual; patients most often report hazy vision or colored halos around lights. Photosensitivity reactions from amiodarone may lead to discoloration (usually slate gray or blue) of periocular skin. A rare adverse effect is amiodarone optic neuropathy, which is characterized by an insidious onset, slow progression, bilateral vision loss, and protracted optic nerve head swelling that tends to stabilize within several months of discontinuing the medi­cation. Patients on long-­term amiodarone therapy should have a baseline ophthalmic examination, follow-up examinations ­every 6–12 months, and immediate evaluation of any new visual disturbances. ­Because of this drug’s photosensitizing effects, UV-­blocking spectacle lenses should be considered in selected cases of chronic eyelid disease or macular disease.
  • b-­Blockers. The use of some b-­blockers can lead to a keratoconjunctivitis sicca–­like syndrome, prob­ably due to decreased lacrimation. They may also possibly enhance migraine ocular scotomata and may decrease intraocular pressure (IOP). Topical b-­blockers, particularly timolol, may be less effective in lowering IOP in patients concurrently taking systemic b-­blockers. Visual disturbances and vivid visual hallucinations may also be associated with the systemic use of b-­blockers.
  • Digoxin. Glare and disturbances of color vision are the most common and striking ocular adverse effects. They include decreased vision and prob­lems with color vision, such as blue-­yellow pattern defects; a yellow, green, blue, or red tinge to objects; and colored halos (mainly blue) around lights. Patients on digoxin may also describe yellow or green flickering vision, colored borders around objects, glare phenomena, light flashes, scintillating scotomata, a frosted appearance to objects, and formed visual hallucinations.
  • ACE inhibitors. ­These agents may cause angioedema involving the eye and orbit. The presumed mechanism is the disruption of bradykinin metabolism.

What percentage of human blood composition is made of formed elements (red blood cells, leukocytes, platelets)?
Correct answer: C. 45%
Rationale
Formed ele­ments—­erythrocytes (red blood cells, or RBCs), leukocytes (white blood cells), and platelets—­constitute approximately 45% of the total blood volume. The fluid portion, plasma, is about 90% ­water. The remaining 10% of the plasma consists of proteins (albumin, globulin, fibrinogen, and enzymes), lipids, carbohydrates, hormones, ­vitamins, and salts. If a blood specimen is allowed to clot, the fibrinogen is consumed, and the resultant fluid portion is called serum.

A 65-year-old woman with symmetric bilateral arthritis presents with ulnar deviation and a positive anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody test. What ocular manifestation is commonly associated with this systemic disease?
Correct answer: C. scleritis and episcleritis
Rationale
The question describes a patient with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Scleritis, episcleritis, and corneal melting are commonly associated with rheumatoid arthritis. Although other areas of the eye can be inflamed in the eyes of patients with RA, these are not typical associations. Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibody has recently been used in the classification of RA and has been shown to be more specific than rheumatoid factor (RF) for the diagnosis of RA. Retinal vasculitis is often seen in antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody (ANCA)-associated diseases, including Behçet disease, but is not common in patients with RA. Optic neuritis is most commonly regarded as clinically isolated or associated with demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis. Trabeculitis may be seen in patients with glaucomatocyclitic crisis or HSV-associated anterior uveitis, but is not typical in patients with RA.

Q6
Update on General Medicine
What is the most radiosensitive structure in the eye?
Correct answer: C. lens
Rationale
In general, radiation and chemotherapy have their greatest effects on mitotically active tissue. As a result, the most radiosensitive structure in the eye is the lens. This is followed, in order, by the cornea, the retina, and the optic nerve. As the retina is not an actively growing tissue, radiation retinopathy can take years to develop and develops from damage to vascular endothelium. Radiation damage may be enhanced by concomitant chemotherapy or concomitant systemic disease states, such as diabetes mellitus or hypertension.

Update on General Medicine
What class of antihypertensive medications should be avoided in patients with reactive airway disease?
Correct answer: D. ß-blockers
Rationale
ß-blockers can cause bronchospasm and should thus be avoided in patients with asthma and reactive airway disease. They also cause bradycardia and should be avoided in patients with second- and third-degree atrioventricular blocks. In patients with heart failure due to systolic dysfunction, the use of certain ß-blockers reduces hospitalization for heart failure and improves survival rates. ß-blockers are beneficial in the treatment of atrial fibrillation and essential tremor.
While ACE inhibitors may cause a dry cough in 5%-20% of patients, reactive airway disease is not a contraindication. ACE inhibitors should be avoided in patients with a history of angioedema or known renal artery stenosis (RAS). They are contraindicated in pregnancy, and in patients trying to become pregnant, because of the adverse effects on fetal renal function and risk of fetal death.
Nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers should be avoided in patients with cardiac conduction abnormalities such as atrial fibrillation or heart failure associated with left ventricular dysfunction and in patients with acute MI.

Update on General Medicine
A 72-year-old man with a past medical history of a myocardial infarction and placement of a cardiac stent 7 months ago presents for preoperative evaluation for cataract surgery. He is taking aspirin and clopidogrel. The surgical plan is for a clear corneal incision approach with topical anesthesia. What is the most appropriate recommendation to the patient for the management of his dual antiplatelet therapy?
Correct answer: B. He may continue taking aspirin and clopidogrel.
Rationale
The decision to maintain or discontinue anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents for ophthalmic surgery is contingent on the type of procedure, the bleeding risk, the effect of bleeding on postoperative outcome, and the risk of a serious or fatal thrombotic event after discontinuation. For uncomplicated cataract surgery via clear corneal incision under topical anesthesia, the benefit of discontinuing anticoagulants to prevent bleeding does not outweigh the potential risks.
Studies found an increased risk of ophthalmic bleeding when warfarin was continued in patients undergoing cataract surgery, but the bleeding events were minor and did not impact vision. Continuation of aspirin or clopidogrel had similar findings. Direct-acting anticoagulants (eg, factor Xa inhibitors [apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban] and a thrombin inhibitor [dabigatran]) have a 20% lower risk than warfarin of causing intraocular bleeding during surgery. In an emergent situation, the monoclonal antibody idarucizumab can reverse the effect of dabigatran within 15 minutes. In patients on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel) after cardiac stent placement, there is increased risk of thrombosis of the stented artery, with potentially fatal outcome if these agents are discontinued.
Cataract surgery may be performed in these patients when cessation of anticoagulants is felt to be unnecessary. For other ocular surgeries in which a successful outcome may be impacted by intraocular or periocular bleeding, some surgeons prefer to stop therapy; in those cases, elective surgery may need to be delayed for up to 1 year to avoid adverse thrombotic events.

A 28-year-old woman has intermediate uveitis, which reactivates with associated macular edema, reducing visual acuity below 20/40 every time her prednisone dosage is reduced below 15 mg per day. She recently learned that she is pregnant. From the perspective of systemic health, what option represents the safest way to manage the intermediate uveitis?
Correct answer: B. periocular injections of triamcinolone acetonide every 2.5 months
Rationale
There is an almost complete absence of randomized clinical trials to guide decision making in the case of a pregnant or lactating patient. However, the best evidence from case series, animal studies, and expert opinion favors a general approach of utilizing the lowest dosage of the least toxic medication available. In a patient with uveitis, use of a local corticosteroid in the form of drops or injections would result in the least amount of medication exposure to the fetus.
Systemic corticosteroid use would result in greater exposure, although this approach can be successful and is often utilized with careful supervision by the obstetrician.
Antimetabolites, such as methotrexate, are known teratogens and are absolutely contraindicated during pregnancy or in those who may soon become pregnant. Similarly, alkylating agents, such as cyclophosphamide, are absolutely contraindicated during pregnancy, and the potential negative effects on fertility should be taken into consideration in all patients of child-bearing age.

A college freshman is brought to the emergency department with fever, headache, neck stiffness, and altered mental status. The patient's lower extremities demonstrate diffuse petechiae. What antibiotic prophylaxis is most appropriate to prescribe for contacts of the patient for the most likely causative organism of this clinical presentation?
Correct answer: D. rifampin
Rationale
This vignette is alluding to bacterial meningitis caused by the organism Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcus). Meningococcus is a gram-negative diplococcus. In young adults, serogroup B is the most common pathogen, which can cause epidemics, especially among close-quartered populations. The high virulence of this organism is partially enabled by the polysaccharide capsule and endotoxin within its cell wall. While the treatment for the infection is classically high-dose penicillin, rifampin or minocycline are typically used for chemoprophylaxis for those that have been in contact with the patient. One of the unique ocular side effects of rifampin is pink-tinged tears, in addition to blepharoconjunctivitis.
Clindamycin is primarily used to treat anaerobic infections and is useful for its coverage of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Cefalexin is a first-generation cephalosporin that is often used to treat skin, bone and joint, and genitourinary infections. Ciprofloxacin is a second-generation fluoroquinolone antibiotic that is often used to treat gastrointestinal and genitourinary infections.

Update on General Medicine
Nearly all isolates of Haemophilus influenzae are now resistant to what antibiotic class?
Correct answer: A. macrolides
Rationale
In general, the treatment of Haemophilus influenzae has been complicated by the emergence of resistant strains. Nearly all strains of H influenzae are resistant to macrolides, and more recent reports note decreased sensitivity to cephalosporins and quinolones. In some geographic regions, ampicillin resistance to H influenzae is nearing 50%.
The most current recommendation for empiric treatment of H influenzae are third- generation cephalosporins, which are highly effective against H influenzae infections. Alternative treatments include meropenem or ampicillin and chloramphenicol. Sensitivity testing should guide further treatment.
Recall, H influenzae is a common inhabitant of the upper respiratory tract in 20% to 50% of adults and 80% of children. Encapsulated species, with increased protection against phagocytosis, are more capable of systemic spread. There are 6 serotypes, with a conjugate vaccination available to type b (Hib). In fully immunized populations, Hib has been nearly eradicated with a significant decline in the rates of meningitis, orbital cellulitis, and other Hib-related infections (arthritis, otitis media, bronchitis, etc).

Update on General Medicine
What is the sensitivity of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing to support the clinical diagnosis in the early stages of Lyme disease?
Correct answer: D. ELISA testing will be only 50% sensitive during the early stages of Lyme disease.
Rationale
Lyme disease, which is caused by the spirochete Borrelia burgdorferi, occurs in 3 stages: Skin manifestations such as erythema chronicum migrans represent the initial, localized disease (stage 1). Disseminated disease (stage 2) manifests as flulike illness. Persistent disease (stage 3) consists of nervous and cardiac involvement. Serologic testing such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) should be used to support the clinical diagnosis and not as a primary basis for treatment. ELISA testing is 50% sensitive in the early stages; however, almost all patients test positive in later stages.
Treatment of B burgdorferi infection depends on the stage and severity of the infection. Early Lyme disease is typically treated with oral doxycycline, amoxicillin, cefuroxime, or erythromycin. Mild disseminated disease is treated with oral doxycycline or amoxicillin. Serious disease (with cardiac or neurologic manifestations) is typically treated with ceftriaxone or high-dose penicillin G for up to 6 weeks.

What virus is the most common reason for liver transplants in the United States?
Correct answer: D. hepatitis C
Rationale
At present, hepatitis C virus (HCV) is the most common cause of liver cancer and the most common indication for liver transplantation in the United States. Because of direct hepatocyte cytotoxicity, HCV causes the most damage of all the hepatitis viruses in immunocompetent hosts. It may cause cirrhosis, fulminant hepatitis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.
Hepatitis A is the leading cause of hepatitis in the US; it usually does not cause long-term liver damage. Hepatitis B can cause cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, which kill 20% and 5% of patients, respectively. Hepatitis D causes chronic delta hepatitis, which is a severe form of chronic liver disease.

What are hallmarks of schizophrenia?
Correct answer: C. hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking
Rationale
The hallmarks of schizophrenia include hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, and negative symptoms such as emotional and cognitive blunting and social and occupational dysfunction. Motor disturbances range from uncontrolled, aimless activity to catatonic stupor, in which the patient is immobile, mute, and unresponsive yet fully conscious. Repetitive, purposeless mannerisms and an inability to complete goal-directed tasks are also common. Patients might have other mental health conditions such as depression or anxiety disorders, but these are not essential features of the disease.

A 73-year-old man presents with a mild preseptal cellulitis of the left lower eyelid, for which he is prescribed oral clindamycin. Two days later, the patient is seen in follow-up, with significant improvement in eyelid erythema and edema. Five days later, the patient calls the ophthalmologist's office complaining of fever and non-bloody diarrhea. What diagnostic test would be most appropriate?
Correct answer: A. stool enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Rationale
This vignette presents a case of Clostridium difficile colitis ("C-diff"). The causative organism is an anaerobic, gram-positive bacillus, which is spore-forming and part of the normal gastrointestinal flora. C-diff commonly occurs in the setting of antibiotic use, as in this case of preseptal cellulitis treated with clindamycin. This antibiotic diarrhea features fever and abdominal discomfort, and the diarrhea may be watery or occasionally bloody. Clindamycin is a common offending antibiotic, along with cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines. The bacterium elaborates cytopathic toxins (A and B), which can be detected by ELISA, and is typically treated with metronidazole or oral vancomycin. A polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay is also sometimes used in the diagnosis of Clostridium difficile colitis.
Stool osmolality, ova and parasites, and culture are all used in the work-up of diarrhea, but in the setting of fever and recent antibiotic usage, C difficile colitis is the most likely diagnosis.

Elevated blood pressure, elevated levels of fasting plasma glucose, elevated triglyceride levels, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels, and what other finding characterize the diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome?

A 20-year-old patient with a history of seizures presents for a general eye examination. During the examination, the patient loses consciousness, and on subsequent electroencephalogram (EEG) testing with her neurologist is found to have abnormal activity in both hemispheres. What type of seizure did the patient experience in the ophthalmologist's office?
Correct answer: C. generalized
Rationale
Generalized seizures cause impaired consciousness and abnormal activity in both hemispheres of the brain at the onset of the seizure. They can be nonconvulsive (absence, or "petit mal") or convulsive (tonic-clonic, or "grand mal"). They may follow a partial seizure. Partial seizures are limited to only 1 part of the brain and further subdivided into simple (without impairment of consciousness) and complex (with impairment of consciousness) seizures. Simple partial seizures are associated with symptomatic auras. Adults with epilepsy most commonly experience complex partial seizures. During these seizures, the patients may appear awake but do not follow instructions or interact.

Update on General Medicine
A 65-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension is brought to the emergency department 2 hours after the onset of weakness on the right side. What imaging study is the most important in guiding acute management?
Correct answer: D. noncontrast head computed tomography (CT)
Rationale
Multiple studies have shown that the administration of the fibrinolytic agent recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) within a 3-hour window of symptom onset results in lower morbidity from ischemic strokes. Imaging with noncontrast head computed tomography (CT) is critical in evaluating for the presence of hemorrhage, which would be a contraindication to rtPA use.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is more sensitive than CT in detecting evolving ischemia within hours of onset, particularly if diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) MRI with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping is used. However, MRI is too time-intensive, considering the limited window during which rtPA can be administered. Cerebral angiography provides detailed information about vascular abnormalities, but it is not indicated in the acute setting and would not reveal either stroke or hemorrhage. Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography may reveal stenosis of the intracranial arteries but is not helpful in making decisions regarding initial management.

What study is preferred to confirm that retinopathy is associated with carotid occlusive disease?
Correct answer: B. computed tomography angiography (CTA)
Rationale
Carotid duplex ultrasonography is relatively inexpensive, quick, and noninvasive, with high sensitivity and high specificity for diagnosing high-grade carotid stenosis. However, ultrasonography may overestimate the degree of stenosis and is less accurate in individuals with less than 69% stenosis. The accuracy of the results is also very much operator-dependent, resulting in high variability among different ultrasound laboratories. If the ultrasonography findings suggest the need for surgical intervention, it may be prudent to confirm with a second imaging modality.
Magnetic resonance angiography is more expensive than carotid ultrasonography and cannot be done in patients who are unable to assume a supine position or in those who have ferromagnetic implants and/or pacemakers. However, the 3-dimensional image of the carotid artery produced by MRA is useful in diagnosing high-grade carotid stenosis and provides an anatomic complement to carotid ultrasonography.
Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is superior to carotid ultrasonography for differentiating high-grade carotid stenosis from total occlusion and effectively excludes carotid stenosis that is greater than 70%, making CTA useful as a screening test. If there is disagreement between MRA and carotid ultrasonography results, CTA is useful in adjudicating the findings. Cerebral angiography remains the gold standard imaging modality for patients with suspected carotid occlusive disease, but its invasive nature and associated morbidity and mortality risk limits its applicability.

Milder changes found earlier in the course of the disease include small-vessel dilation and microaneurysm; more severe ischemia may result in peripheral retinal nonperfusion, iris and retinal nonperfusion, iris and retinal neovascularization, and vitreous hemorrhage. It is most common in Asian or African females in their 20s. Diminished radial pulses and systemic hypertension can help diagnose the condition, but angiography is diagnostic.
Giant cell arteritis with temporal artery tenderness can also cause posterior-circulation stroke but it is found in patients over 50 years old. Fusion of the sacroiliac joint suggests ankylosing spondylitis. Facial hirsutism and irregular menses imply hypothalamic-pituitary axis abnormalities.

Sildenafil and tadalafil have been shown to be effective in treating pulmonary arterial hypertension. They also may increase the risk of nonarteritic ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) in high-risk patients. Individuals may be taking this class of medications for the treatment of what disorder?
Correct answer: A. erectile dysfunction
Rationale
Sildenafil and tadalafil belong to a class of drugs called phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitors. They prevent degradation of cGMP and promote blood flow. This class of medications is well-known for the treatment of erectile dysfunction, but they also can be effective in treating pulmonary arterial hypertension. There also has been an association of NAION in high-risk patients.

Update on General Medicine
What extraocular muscle is most commonly injured by a retrobulbar injection?
Correct answer: C. inferior rectus
Rationale
The EOMs can be damaged from retrobulbar injections, either by direct injury to muscles or from toxicity of the injected material. Because of the usual site of these injections, the vertical rectus muscles are the most vulnerable.
