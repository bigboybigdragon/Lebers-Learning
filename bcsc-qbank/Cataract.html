A patient has a white cataract. In addition to the standard supplies and instruments for cataract surgery, what should the surgeon have ready for use in this patient?
Correct answer: D. trypan blue dye
Rationale
The surgeon should always be prepared for likely complications by having alternative surgical plans ready and the appropriate instruments available in the operating room. Staining the capsule of a white cataract aids in direct visualization and promotes creation of a controlled continuous curvilinear capsulorrhexis (CCC). Trypan blue dye would certainly be advantageous for capsular staining. If a pupil is miotic or there is zonular weakness, the surgeon should be ready to use other devices as the situation dictates.

Q3216
Lens & Cataract
What postoperative complication may result from the creation of a 7.0 mm capsulorrhexis during cataract surgery?
Correct answer: D. IOL dislocation
Rationale
The creation of an overly large (7.0 mm)  capsulorrhexis is a well-known risk factor for IOL dislocation. Other factors include haptic damage, zonular defects, or placement of a 1-piece IOL in the sulcus. Capsular block syndrome occurs when OVD remains posterior to the IOL, causing an anterior shift of the IOL and myopic shift, but it is not associated with a large capsulorrhexis. IOL glistenings have been implicated in piggybacking lenses, in which a large capsulorrhexis may reduce the incidence. Capsular phimosis occurs with a small capsulorrhexis, not a large one.

Q3217
Lens & Cataract
The risk of what intraoperative occurrence is increased during phacoemulsification of the type of cataract demonstrated in the figure?
Correct answer: B. posterior capsular rupture
Rationale
A weak or absent area of posterior capsule in a posterior polar cataract increases the risk of capsular rupture during cataract surgery. For this reason, avoiding complete hydrodissection in order to avoid excessive pressure build-up and possible tearing of the capsule beneath the opacity may be advisable. Gentle hydrodelineation using small volumes of fluid may be preferable. It is also recommended to remove epinuclear and/or cortical material in the vicinity of the posterior polar opacity last in the event of absent posterior capsule in this area. In some situations, it may be safer to leave a firmly adherent posterior polar opacity and treat this area postoperatively with a Nd:YAG capsulotomy.
There is no associated increased risk of iris prolapse, poor pupillary dilation, or capsulorrhexis extension among patients with posterior polar cataracts.

Q3218
Lens & Cataract
During cataract surgery there is a large break in the posterior capsule. The decision is made to place an intraocular lens (IOL) in the ciliary sulcus. Why should a 3-piece IOL be used instead of a single-piece acrylic IOL?
Correct answer: C. The larger haptics on the single-piece acrylic IOL can make contact with uveal tissues and lead to uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema (UGH) syndrome.
Rationale
A single-piece acrylic IOL has large haptics that can make contact with uveal tissues, causing uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema (UGH) syndrome. Damage to the zonules and late dislocation due to the haptics has not been reported.
Single-piece acrylic IOLs  are planar and not posteriorly vaulted like 3-piece IOLs. However, any IOL placed in the sulcus without optic capture will have more effective refracting power. Therefore, the power of the IOL should be decreased. If one is planning to capture the optic in the capsulorhexis, the IOL power should not be changed.
Single-piece acrylic IOLs are very flexible, and optic capture could easily be performed. Because of the issue of the haptics in the sulcus, however, it is not advisable to do so. Some surgeons have described a "reverse optic capture," where the single-piece acrylic haptics are placed into the capsular bag and the optic is brought forward through the capsulorhexis. This is useful if the capsule is broken, but the surgeon still wants to use the single-piece acrylic IOL, such as when a multifocal IOL was planned. It has also been described as a technique to reduce negative dysphotopsia after cataract surgery.

Q3219
Lens & Cataract
What is the mechanism for hypotony following a traumatic cyclodialysis cleft?
Correct answer: A. increased uveoscleral outflow
Rationale
Cyclodialysis is the separation of the ciliary body from its insertion at the scleral spur. It can occur as a result of trauma or of surgical manipulation of intraocular tissue. Repair of a cyclodialysis cleft is often indicated to relieve prolonged hypotony. Hypotony is caused by the increased uveoscleral outflow because it is anatomically a separation at its insertion, not an increase in trabecular outflow. Aqueous misdirection is a cause of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), not hypotony. Uveitis can sometimes cause increased IOP and sometimes decreased IOP, but generally not enough to cause hypotony.

Q3220
Lens & Cataract
What systemic disorder increases the likelihood of developing a posterior subcapsular cataract?
Correct answer: C. diabetes mellitus
Rationale
Diabetes mellitus is associated with accelerated development of all age-related lens changes, including posterior subcapsular cataract. The proposed pathophysiologic reasons for these changes are multifactorial, including increased lens hydration from sorbitol accumulation, glycation of lens proteins, and increased oxidative stress. Systemic hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and ischemic cardiovascular disease are not associated with increased risk for developing posterior subcapsular cataract, although these conditions may be relevant for evaluation of general health and for preoperative planning for cataract surgery.

What method would be useful in distinguishing the effect of corneal disease on visual acuity in a patient with both anterior basement membrane dystrophy and cataract?
Correct answer: A. gas-permeable contact lens
Rationale
The thickened epithelium and redundant basement membrane of anterior basement membrane dystrophy, when present in the visual axis, results in irregular corneal astigmatism. A trial of gas-permeable contact lens can mask this irregular astigmatism and help determine its effect on visual impairment.

Q3224
Lens & Cataract
On the first day following uncomplicated phacoemulsification, an increase in intraocular pressure (IOP) is noted. What is the most likely etiology?
Correct answer: C. retained viscoelastic
Rationale
A mild, self-limited rise in IOP is common following cataract surgery. However, a significant and sustained elevation may require timely management. Ophthalmic viscosurgical devices (OVDs) retained in the eye after cataract surgery are frequently responsible for early postoperative IOP elevation, which peaks 4 to 6 hours after surgery. The large molecules of the viscoelastic material can impair aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork. Even when all apparent OVD is removed from the anterior chamber, residual OVD, particularly dispersive agents, can lodge in the posterior chamber or behind the lens implant. All types of OVDs, but in particular the higher-viscosity agents, may cause the pressure to rise. IOP elevation usually does not last more than a few days and is amenable to medical treatment. The clinician may quickly manage marked IOP elevation in the early postoperative period by applying gentle pressure on the posterior lip of a preexisting paracentesis incision to release a small amount of aqueous humor. Topical and/or systemic pressure-lowering agents should also be administered, as IOP reduction after aqueous release is short-lived, with the IOP likely to rise again within 1 to 2 hours of the decompression.
Elevated IOP without angle closure after cataract surgery may be caused by hyphema, toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS), endophthalmitis, retained lens material (phacolytic or phacoantigenic [formerly termed phacoanaphylactic] reactions), uveitis, iris pigment release, preexisting glaucoma, corticosteroid use, vitreous in the anterior chamber, ghost cell glaucoma, or a-chymotrypsin use. Angle-closure glaucoma may be due to pupillary block, ciliary block, epithelial ingrowth, neovascular glaucoma, or peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS). Treating the underlying cause of the IOP elevation should be curative.

Q3225
Lens & Cataract
How does toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS) differ in presentation from other causes of postoperative inflammation?
Correct answer: A. TASS is more likely to present at postoperative day one.
Rationale
The symptoms and signs of TASS may mimic those of infectious endophthalmitis and include photophobia, severe reduction in visual acuity, corneal edema, and marked anterior chamber reaction, occasionally with hypopyon. TASS presents within hours of surgery, whereas acute infectious endophthalmitis typically develops 2 to 7 days postoperatively. Other potentially distinguishing features of TASS include diffuse, limbus-to-limbus corneal edema; anterior chamber fibrinous exudate; a dilated, irregular, or nonreactive pupil; and elevated IOP. The pathologic changes are limited to the anterior chamber. Pain is typically much milder than that experienced with an infection. If endophthalmitis is suspected, diagnostic and therapeutic interventions should be undertaken.

A patient with a history of radial keratotomy (RK) undergoes cataract surgery. What potential issue should the surgeon anticipate?
Correct answer: B. intraoperative anterior chamber shallowing
Rationale
RK incisions typically swell after cataract surgery, causing flattening of the cornea and inducing hyperopia. If the surgeon chooses to make a corneal cataract incision, it should be placed between RK incisions. Violating an old RK incision can destabilize the wound. Multiple deep RK incisions increase the likelihood of anterior chamber shallowing during cataract surgery. Prior RK should not cause loss of the red reflex or affect the rate of cystoid macular edema.

Q3227
Lens & Cataract
In addition to elongation and movement toward the bow region of the lens, what changes occur to lens epithelial cells during terminal differentiation?
Correct answer: D. an increase in cell protein content and loss of organelles
Rationale
Terminal differentiation is a process by which a precursor cell that was formerly capable of cell division permanently leaves the cell cycle. In lens cells, terminal differentiation involves elongation of the lens epithelial cells into lens fibers. This change is associated with a tremendous increase in the mass of cellular proteins in the fiber cell membrane. The cells lose organelles, including nuclei, mitochondria, and ribosomes. The loss of cell organelles is optically advantageous; however, the cells then become more dependent on glycolysis for energy production and become less active metabolically.

An ophthalmologist is performing phacoemulsification surgery on a patient with a dense, brunescent cataract. What nuclear disassembly technique would be the most likely to result in complications such as zonular stress or endothelial damage?
Correct answer: A. divide and conquer
Rationale
In eyes with dense, brunescent cataracts, surgical manipulation increases the risks of iris trauma, zonular tearing, capsular rupture, vitreous loss, and the dropping of lens fragments into the posterior segment. The increased ultrasound energy required for phacoemulsification of dense lenses increases the risk of endothelial trauma and wound burn. Mechanical segmentation techniques for nucleus disassembly, such as vertical and horizontal chopping, require less ultrasound energy and may induce less zonular stress than the “divide and conquer” method.

Q3232
Lens & Cataract
What is characteristic of the diagnosis or treatment of clinical cystoid macular edema (CME) following cataract extraction?
Correct answer: D. Cystoid macular edema is seen more frequently in cases that have vitreomacular traction.
Rationale
The prevalence of clinically apparent cystoid macular edema following cataract extraction is 1.2% to 3.5% of cases. Although the pathogenesis of CME is unknown, the final common pathway appears to be increased capillary permeability with accumulation of fluid in the inner nuclear and outer plexiform layers of the retina. CME is often associated with intraocular inflammation and may be mediated through the release of prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Associated intraoperative risk factors include vitreomacular traction, excessive exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light, posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, iris prolapse, and transient or prolonged hypotony.
Clinically apparent cystoid macular edema is defined in contrast to angiographic CME. Angiographic CME occurs in 40% to 70% of eyes following ICCE and in approximately 1% to 19% following extracapsular cataract extraction (ECCE) via nuclear expression or phacoemulsification, and is visible on fluorescein angiography, but not by traditional ophthalmoscopy. Clinically apparent CME can be recognized by the characteristic petaloid appearance of cystic spaces in the macula on ophthalmoscopy and by fluorescein angiography, or by cystic areas of low reflectivity and retinal thickening on spectral domain OCT.
The risk of CME may be reduced with preoperative and postoperative prophylactic use of topical ketorolac or other NSAID eyedrops. Because most cases of postoperative CME resolve spontaneously, it is difficult to assess the effect of these therapeutic agents. In a prospective randomized controlled clinical trial, topical ketorolac 0.5% or prednisolone acetate 1.0% was demonstrated to be effective therapy for chronic CME, but greater improvement in visual acuity was obtained with a combination of ketorolac and prednisolone 4 times per day than with either medication alone. Systemic NSAIDs have not been demonstrated to be effective therapy.

Q3233
Lens & Cataract
What management strategy may be employed to reduce risk of intraoperative or postoperative uveal effusion for cataract surgery on a nanophthalmic eye?
Correct answer: A. suturing all incisions
Rationale
Nanophthalmos is a rare condition in which the eye is extremely short (axial length <20.5 mm) and the ratio of lens volume to eye volume is larger than normal. These eyes have shallow anterior chambers, narrow angles, and thickened sclerae, with little room for the surgeon to maneuver. Small-incision bimanual surgery is an alternative technique to consider. Intraoperative or postoperative uveal effusion is a unique hazard in nanophthalmic eyes. Maintaining positive pressure in the anterior chamber and limiting the length of the procedure aid in preventing intraoperative uveal effusion.
Scleral windows, not additional paracentesis incisions, should be considered as a prophylactic measure to treat uveal effusion. A sutured wound prevents hypotony from contributing to this complication postoperatively.
Other maneuvers, such as elevation of the irrigation bottle and making anterior corneal incisions to minimize iris prolapse, are recommended.

Q3234
Lens & Cataract
A patient with complaints of negative dysphotopsia following otherwise uneventful cataract surgery is referred to an ophthalmologist. What is associated with this patient's symptom?
Correct answer: D. square-optic edge design
Rationale
Negative dysphotopsia is described as an arcuate dark or dim crescent-shaped region, usually in the temporal visual field. It occurs in the setting of a posterior chamber intraocular lens (PCIOL) centered in the capsular bag with the anterior capsule edge overlapping the lens optic. Though reported with most styles of PCIOLs, negative dysphotopsia is more common with smaller, square-edge optic designs. Temporal light rays may interact with the nasal lens edge and anterior capsule, causing a shadow (penumbra) on the nasal retina in susceptible eyes. This effect is more common with a miotic or nasally located pupil and is relieved with dilation or by blocking light from the temporal side.
Although symptoms are common in the early postoperative period, they improve over time in most patients, presumably as the capsule contracts or opacifies in the periphery. Initially, observation is advised. For patients with prolonged symptoms and compromised vision, surgery may be necessary. Repositioning of the optic anterior to the capsulorrhexis by reverse optic capture through the capsulorrhexis (with the haptics in the capsular bag) or sulcus fixation of an appropriate PCIOL with a larger optic with rounded edges may be successful in most cases. Implanting a piggyback IOL or performing an anterior capsulectomy has been successful in some cases.

Q3238
Lens & Cataract
If an attempted continuous curvilinear capsulorrhexis has extended peripherally, what management technique should be applied?
Correct answer: B. deepening of the anterior chamber and flattening of the capsule flap with viscoelastic
Rationale
Maintaining the continuity of a continuous curvilinear capsulorrhexis is paramount to retaining its primary advantage of reducing the risk of tears extending to the posterior capsule during phacoemulsification. Any factor that causes shallowing of the anterior chamber will result in the lens moving forward, which would encourage extension of the capsule tear outward during creation of the capsulorrhexis. Ophthalmic viscosurgical devices (OVDs) should be added to hold the lens back and depress the anterior capsular surface, relieving outward tension on the peripheral anterior capsule by the anterior zonular apparatus.
Aspirating OVDs or enlarging or depressing the wound or the globe will encourage expulsion of OVDs and allow forward movement of the lens and outward pressure on the capsule. Even lightly depressing the central lens posteriorly with an instrument inserted through a sideport incision may also help reduce outward radialization of the tear and allow better inward control.
After maintaining the lens apparatus deeply and posteriorly with OVDs, the most common technique for maximally controlling the tear inward is to regrasp the flap near the end of the tear and slowly re-curve it inward.
An additional innovative technique has been recently described: The flap near the tear terminus is allowed to lie back down in its original position, then the flap is grabbed near the tear terminus and pulled slowly and gently back, as if to be lightly stretched back down into its original position and slightly inward, which will usually result in an abrupt inward turn of the tear.
Conversion to can-opener technique is reasonable, but an attempt should be made to redirect the continuous tear first, due to the increased risk of extension with a can-opener rhexis.

Q3240
Lens & Cataract
What molecular activity in the crystalline lens is most associated with the development of age-related nuclear sclerosis?
Correct answer: C. Protein-to-glutathione disulfide bond formation results in increased protein aggregation.
Rationale
Cataract formation occurs as proteins aggregate to become very large particles that are water-insoluble and scatter light. As a lens ages, associated oxidative changes lead to increased aggregation of proteins by protein-to-protein and protein-to-glutathione disulfide bond formation. Glutathione in its reduced form is seen as an essential antioxidant (to maintain a reducing environment in the lens cytoplasm). When it is depleted by protein-to-glutathione disulfide bond formation, there is accelerated protein crosslinking, protein aggregation, and light scattering.
The other molecular events create the normal proteins that make up the human lens. The lens has at least twice the protein concentration of most other tissues (lens protein concentration: 33% of its wet weight). α-Crystallins are water soluble and represent one-third of the lens proteins. They bind to partially denatured proteins and prevent aggregation, thus inhibiting the complete denaturation and insolubilization of other crystallins.
The other water-soluble proteins are β,γ-Crystallins. Remember cataract formation is associated with increased water-insoluble proteins. β-Crystallins are encoded by 7 genes. Individual polypeptides associate with one another to form dimers and higher-order complexes in their native state. Intermediate filaments are part of the water-insoluble fraction of lens protein; however, they are one component of the structural framework of the lens cells. Thus they are not associated with cataract formation unless there is genetic disruption of their structure, in particular in the beaded filaments, which are composed of phakinin and filensin (proteins specific to the lens).
The other intermediate filament type aside from beaded filaments is one made from the protein vimentin, which is not usually seen in epithelial cells.

Q3241
Lens & Cataract
On examination 9 months following routine uncomplicated cataract extraction, a patient with no prior ocular comorbidities presents with intraocular pressure of 32 mm Hg in the operative eye. Slit-lamp examination is significant for a gray retrocorneal opacification in proximity to the main wound, with associated corneal edema. No lens material is seen in the angle on gonioscopy. What is the most likely mechanism of elevated intraocular pressure in this patient?
Correct answer: A. epithelial obstruction of trabecular meshwork
Rationale
The description of retrocorneal opacification with corneal edema following intraocular surgery is alluding to epithelial ingrowth (also called downgrowth), which is a rare condition characterized by a sheet of epithelium growing intraocularly following trauma or a surgical incision and covering the corneal endothelium, trabecular meshwork, and/or iris surfaces. One of the clinical signs of epithelial downgrowth is an elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). The mechanism for elevated IOP is outflow obstruction caused by the growth of the epithelial membrane over the trabecular meshwork or by epithelial cells clogging the meshwork.
Retained ophthalmic viscosurgical device is a common cause for elevated IOP following cataract surgery, but usually peaks 4 to 6 hours following surgery, and is more typical with higher viscosity agents. Postoperative inflammation is an unlikely mechanism, given that the patient is 9 months out from surgery and the examination does not demonstrate significant anterior chamber inflammation. Posterior misdirection of aqueous is the classically described mechanism for malignant glaucoma or aqueous misdirection, in which secondary angle-closure glaucoma results from anterior rotation of the ciliary body, with resultant posterior misdirection of aqueous humor into the vitreous cavity. Another cause of elevated IOP following cataract surgery is retained anterior chamber or posterior chamber lens material, although in the scenario depicted in the question stem, the result of the gonioscopic examination was negative.

Q3258
Lens & Cataract
What best describes viscosity as a physical property of an ophthalmic viscosurgical device (OVD)?
Correct answer: C. resistance to flow; the thickness and thinness of a fluid
Rationale
Viscosity is resistance to flow and can be thought of as the “thickness” or “thinness” of a fluid. It is determined primarily by molecular weight and concentration, so that substances with high molecular weight and high concentration have the highest viscosity. The higher the viscosity, the better the OVD is at displacing tissue and staying in place.
Elasticity refers to the ability of a material to return to its original shape after being stressed. It describes the OVD’s ability to re‑form after an external force is applied to the anterior chamber and then removed.
Surface tension describes how the surface of a fluid tends to stick to another surface. This property is also referred to as coatability, which is inversely proportional to surface tension. Thus, an OVD with low surface tension is better at coating tissue but is harder to remove from the eye.
Cohesive OVDs are long-chain, high-molecular-weight, high-viscosity substances. These agents maintain space well at no or low shear rates, whereas at high shear rates they are easily displaced. Cohesive OVDs are easier to remove from the eye because they stick together and are aspirated as long pieces (similar to spaghetti). However, they have minimal coating ability and therefore afford less tissue protection during surgery.

Q3284
Lens & Cataract
What is the most active pathway for glucose metabolism in the lens?
Correct answer: C. anaerobic glycolysis
Rationale
Energy production within the lens depends on the metabolism of glucose, which enters the lens through simple and facilitated diffusion from the aqueous humor. The glucose is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) by hexokinase before being transferred into the lens, and this is the rate-limiting step of glucose metabolism in the lens. Anaerobic glycolysis utilizes about 80% of glucose processes for energy in the lens. Less than 4% is normally converted to sorbitol. Less than 5% is metabolized by the HMP pathway. Only about 3% of the lens glucose passes through the Krebs cycle; however, even this low level of aerobic metabolism produces approximately 25% of the ATP of the lens. One glucose molecule will create 2 adenosine triphosphate (ATP) molecules via anaerobic glycolysis.
Under conditions of very high concentrations of sugar, the sorbitol pathway is activated, converting glucose to sorbitol and then fructose. This pathway then competes with anaerobic glycolysis for glucose. Aldose reductase is the enzyme in this pathway that converts glucose to sorbitol, and it is activated only when glucose concentrations are very high. Therefore, "sugar cataracts" are thought to develop when high concentrations of glucose activate aldose reductase, which increases the lens concentration of sorbitol. Sorbitol then osmotically drives water into the lens and causes cataract formation.
The hexose monophosphate shunt (pentose phosphate shunt) is activated under times of oxidative stress because it replenishes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NAPDH), which is oxidized by the glutathione reductase pathway in times of oxidative stress. Only 3% of the glucose is metabolized directly by the Krebs cycle. Pyruvate is the major substrate used for ATP production by the Krebs cycle.

A Mittendorf dot is a remnant of the hyaloid vasculature located where?
Correct answer: A. on the posterior pole of the lens
Rationale
During embryogenesis, the hyaloid artery supplies nutrients to the developing lens. The hyaloid vasculature usually disappears prior to birth. However, there are cases wherein this regression is not complete. Occasionally, remnants of the hyaloid vasculature can be seen either on the posterior pole of the lens (Mittendorf dot) or on the optic nerve (Bergmeister papilla). There may also be opacities left in the vitreous. Regression of the hyaloid vasculature also leaves a channel through the vitreous (Cloquet canal).

Q3473
Lens & Cataract
The majority of high-energy phosphate bonds required for lens metabolism are provided by what pathway?
Correct answer: A. anaerobic glycolysis
Rationale
The goal of lens metabolism is the maintenance of lens transparency. In the lens, energy production largely depends on glucose metabolism. Glucose enters the lens from the aqueous humor both by simple diffusion and by a mediated transfer process called facilitated diffusion. Most of the glucose transported into the lens is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) by the enzyme hexokinase. Once formed, G6P enters 1 of 2 metabolic pathways: anaerobic glycolysis or the hexose monophosphate (HMP) shunt. The more active of these 2 pathways is anaerobic glycolysis, which provides most of the high-energy phosphate bonds required for lens metabolism. This pathway is much less efficient than aerobic glycolysis, because only 2 net molecules of ATP are produced for each glucose molecule utilized, whereas aerobic glycolysis produces an additional 36 molecules of ATP from each glucose molecule metabolized in the citric acid cycle (oxidative metabolism). Because of the low oxygen tension in the lens, only about 3% of the lens glucose passes through the citric acid cycle (also called the tricarboxylic acid cycle or the Krebs cycle) to produce ATP; however, even this low level of aerobic metabolism produces approximately 25% of the lens ATP.

Q3475
Lens & Cataract
What operative factor contributes to microscope-induced light toxicity during ocular surgery?
Correct answer: C. long operating time
Rationale
Longer operating times contribute to photic injury. Microscope filters and tilting the microscope reduce the risk of photic injury. It has not been shown or claimed that the use of viscoelastic significantly reduces photic injury. Prolonged exposure to the illuminating filament of the operating microscope can result in an increased risk of cystoid macular edema or a burn to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The risk of an RPE burn is particularly high during cataract surgery, when the light-absorbing effects of the natural lens (cataract) are removed. This maneuver exposes the RPE to minimally attenuated blue and near-ultraviolet radiation. If the photic injury occurs in or adjacent to the fovea, visual acuity may be reduced. If the injury is extrafoveal, the patient may develop a paracentral scotoma. Minimizing retinal exposure to the operating microscope light, and keeping operating times as short as possible, help reduce this complication. Other actions to reduce the risk of retinal photic injury include using the minimum light intensity needed to safely perform the procedure, always using manufacturer-approved lamps, using a filter to attenuate wavelengths below 515 nm, using oblique lighting when possible, using pupillary shields, and minimizing direct exposure of the fovea.
