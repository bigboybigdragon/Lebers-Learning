What postoperative complications can occur with laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), but do not occur with corneal implanted devices such as intrastromal corneal ring segments (ICRSs)?
Correct answer: A. corneal ectasia
Rationale
Irregular astigmatism and infectious keratitis can occur after both LASIK and intrastromal corneal ring segment implantation. Implant extrusion would only occur after segment implantation. Ectasia is a complication of LASIK, and intrastromal corneal ring segments are one potential treatment for ectasia.

Q3846
Refractive Surgery
In the setting of refractive surgery, what are the typical consequences of significant higher-order aberrations?
Correct answer: C. symptoms of higher-order aberrations include glare, halos, or ghosting
Rationale
Patients with higher-order aberrations usually notice glare, halos, or ghosting. Lower-order aberrations (ie, spherocylindrical aberrations) can be treated with glasses or contact lenses and include myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism. The symptoms are more apparent with dilation of the pupil. Higher-order aberrations cannot be treated with glasses, but contact lenses can reduce these aberrations. Coma, trefoil, and spherical aberration are examples of higher-order aberrations. Both lower- and higher-order aberrations can be treated with the excimer laser. Lower-order aberrations typically make up 90% of a patient's overall aberration profile while higher-order aberrations make up the remaining 10%. Several studies have shown that mechanical microkeratomes worsen higher-order aberrations while the femtosecond laser does not worsen them. In any event, LASIK flap creation does not improve higher-order aberrations.

Q3849
Refractive Surgery
What advantage does the femtosecond laser have compared to a mechanical microkeratome?
Correct answer: D. better control of the construction of the flap
Rationale
The femtosecond laser has several advantages over the mechanical microkeratome but the only one offered is that it provides better control over the construction of the flap. Variables such as flap location, diameter, thickness, and hinge location are able to be fine-tuned with the femtosecond laser. The frequency of other flap complications such as a free cap or buttonhole flap are reduced or eliminated. The femtosecond laser has not been shown to reduce the incidence of bacterial keratitis or diffuse lamellar keratitis and has not been shown to produce better visual acuity.

Q3850
Refractive Surgery
What is the most common adverse effect resulting from dry eyes after LASIK?
Correct answer: D. decreased vision
Rationale
Dry-eye symptoms are the most common adverse effects of refractive surgery and occasionally persist for months or years; they have been reported in 60%–70% of all patients, to varying degrees. Corneal sensitivity decreases after LASIK because of the surgical amputation of nerves during flap formation and the destruction of superficial nerve fibers during the laser ablation. This can result in corneal anesthesia lasting 3–6 months. As a result, most patients experience a decrease in tear production. Patients who had dry eyes before surgery or whose eyes were marginally compensated before surgery might have more severe symptoms. In addition, patients with dry eyes following LASIK or surface ablation have an abnormal tear film and a poor ocular surface and they often note fluctuating vision between blinks and at different times of the day.

Q3851
Refractive Surgery
How should microstriae in a patient with excellent vision on the first day after laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) be treated?
Correct answer: A. observation
Rationale
Microstriae are fine, hairlike optical irregularities corresponding to fine folds in the Bowman layer, which can be seen on retroillumination. Management of microstriae depends on whether they affect visual acuity. If they do not affect vision, they can be observed. If they are visually significant, both suturing the flap and phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK) are potential options for treatment.
Flap floating with and without hypertonic saline are options to treat visually significant macrostriae.

Q3852
Refractive Surgery
How is the risk of retinal detachment changed by laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), photorefractive keratectomy (PRK), or radial keratotomy?
Correct answer: C. Retinal detachment risk is unaffected after any of these refractive procedures.
Rationale
Patients with high myopia should be counseled that refractive surgery corrects only the refractive aspect of the myopia and not the natural history of the highly myopic eye, with its known complications. Highly myopic patients remain at risk for retinal tears and detachment throughout their lives, despite refractive surgery. No causal link has been established between retinal detachment and excimer laser refractive surgery. Current data (albeit not well-controlled, long-term studies) suggest that surface ablation, radial keratotomy, and LASIK do not appear to increase the incidence of retinal detachment. Symptomatic retinal tears or subclinical retinal detachments should always be treated. Tears or holes associated with high myopia should be considered for treatment.
Retinal detachment repair may interfere with a previous LASIK flap; if corneal scraping during surgery is required, the flap may be disrupted, and the retinal surgeon should be made aware of prior LASIK before retinal detachment repair is undertaken.

Q3857
Refractive Surgery
What is the minimal thickness recommended for the residual stromal bed (RSB) following laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK)?
Correct answer: B. 250 µm
Rationale
The strength of the cornea after laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) is greatly dependent on the residual stromal bed (RSB). RSB is calculated by taking the central corneal thickness and subtracting the presumed flap thickness and ablation depth. The ablation depth is determined by the patient’s actual refraction and non-nomogram-adjusted number used by physicians using the VISX laser. A RSB of at least 250 µm is recommended to avoid potential postoperative weakness in the cornea that may manifest as corneal ectasia.
Some surgeons are even more conservative and use 275 µm or 300 µm as their minimum stromal bed thickness. Some surgeons also use 500 µm as their minimum preoperative corneal thickness to reduce the risk of postoperative corneal ectasia.

Q3859
Refractive Surgery
Larger optical zones and treatment areas reduce what complication of hyperopic refractive surgery?
Correct answer: D. degree of glare
Rationale
Glare is a possible complication of both hyperopic and myopic excimer laser photoablation and can result from an abrupt transition between the optical zone and the untreated cornea. Because the sculpting to create a steeper central cornea in hyperopic treatments occurs with ablation of the midperipheral cornea, it is important to plan for, and program into the laser, an appropriate blend zone in hyperopic treatments to minimize an abrupt transition (figure). A prolate blend zone reduces glare and haloes following excimer laser photo ablation.
Regression of effect and residual refractive error can occur no matter what preoperative considerations and measures are employed. Use of nomograms is the primary manner in which final residual refractive error is adjusted, not through changing the optical zone. In general, patients with higher degrees of myopia and any degree of hyperopia require more time to attain refractive stability, which must be achieved prior to considering retreatment.
Postoperative discomfort can be managed effectively with oral analgesics and steroidal and nonsteroidal topical drops, as well as bandage contact lenses after surface ablations. Intraoperatively, chilled balanced salt solution (BSS) can be used to decrease postoperative discomfort. No effective preoperative method has been found to prevent discomfort postoperatively.
A larger optical zone and therefore more tissue ablation would increase the risk of postoperative ectasia. Ectasia is typically higher risk in LASIK, and in patients with thin corneas or higher myopic corrections or who have undergone several laser ablations. However, cases of ectasia without any demonstrable risk factors have also been reported.

Immediately following cataract surgery with a diffractive multifocal intraocular lens (IOL), a patient complains of visually disabling glare and halos that persist over the next several months. What is the best next step in management?
Correct answer: C. IOL exchange with a monofocal IOL
Rationale
All multifocal lenses have the potential of causing halos. Many patients may experience fewer halos over several months, potentially from neural adaptation. However, should halos be disabling and persistent, a lens exchange may be the only option. A piggyback IOL would not address the halos.

While patients with multifocal IOLs may be more sensitive to posterior capsule opacification (PCO), symptoms from PCO are typically not present initially but gradually worsen over the first few weeks to months after surgery. In contrast, intrinsic IOL symptoms appear early, if not immediately, in the postoperative course and stay consistent over time. Additionally, performing a capsulotomy would make future lens exchanges more difficult.

A 45-year-old patient with myopia desires monovision correction with LASIK. The nondominant eye is chosen for near vision and has a refraction of –5.00 sphere. Assuming no nomogram adjustment is required, what is the most appropriate laser treatment setting?
Correct answer: A. –3.50 sphere
Rationale
To create monovision in a myopic patient, it is necessary to undercorrect 1 eye, usually the nondominant eye. The amount of desired undercorrection is determined by a combination of the patient's requirements for near vision and the amount of anisometropia that is tolerable. In a 45-year-old patient, 1.50 D is generally the appropriate amount of undercorrection for providing functional near vision without generating significant problems with anisometropia if the nondominant eye requires correction with glasses for activities such as driving at night. All patients being evaluated for a surgical monovision correction should initially try monovision contact lenses to ensure they can tolerate the anisometropia and visual quality of monovision and to confirm the preferred distance and near eye.

Q3871
Refractive Surgery
What does the term coupling refer to within refractive surgery?
Correct answer: D. the flattening of a cornea in the meridian of a limbal relaxing incision with concurrent steepening in the meridian 90° away
Rationale
Tangential and limbal relaxing corneal incisions alter the shape of the cornea by inducing flattening in the meridian of the incision. At the same time, there is a resultant steepening of the cornea 90° away from the incision, an effect termed coupling. With limbal relaxing incisions, the amount of steepening is equal to the amount of flattening, which results in a coupling ratio of 1.0 and no shift in the refractive error (figure). On the other hand, transverse incisions result in overall flattening of the cornea, a hyperopic shift, and a coupling ratio of greater than 1.0. Radial incisions result in localized flattening without steepening 90° away. A double running suture in a corneal transplant does not result in coupling, nor does photorefractive keratectomy.

Corneal topographic measurements obtained using Placido disk technology can be adversely affected by what disorder?
Correct answer: A. dry eye
Rationale

Q3874

What is the optimal refractive procedure for patients with epithelial basement membrane dystrophy?
Correct answer: D. photorefractive keratectomy (PRK)
Rationale
There are certain situations in which photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is preferred over LASIK or other refractive procedures. One of these is a patient with epithelial basement membrane dystrophy. Q3875
Refractive Surgery
Why might increasing the optical zone in myopic laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) or photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) limit the ability to treat patients with thinner corneas?
Correct answer: C. It increases the ablation depth.
Rationale
All photoablative procedures result in the removal of corneal tissue. The depth of myopic laser ablation by a broad beam laser is estimated by the Munnerlyn formula.
Ablation depth (microns) = [degree of myopia (D) x (optical zone diameter in mm)^2]/3
As the ablation diameter increases, the depth required for a given refractive correction increases by the optical zone squared. Thus, the difference in the ablation depth increase is not linear and can rapidly exceed the ability of the cornea to structurally handle the ablation depth and still maintain appropriate residual stromal bed thickness. Complications of glare, halos, and regression increase when the optical zone decreases. So increasing the optical zone would actually improve these issues. For this reason, the optical zone should be 6 mm or larger.

Q3879
Refractive Surgery
What wavelength of energy, when applied to the cornea, is characterized by high energy and precision, low penetration of tissue, and limited thermal spread?
Correct answer: A. 193 nm
Rationale
A variety of laser-tissue interactions are used in keratorefractive surgery. Argon-fluoride excimer laser generates a wavelength of 193 nm with high energy and precision but low tissue penetrance and little thermal spread, making this laser highly suitable for use on the ocular surface. This is useful in the creation of corneal flattening at the anterior surface, and this technique (laser thermokeratoplasty [LTK]) is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of low hyperopia. Collagen cross-linking units create a wavelength of 365 nm (ultraviolet A) that, while not used in refractive surgery, is used the treatment of corneal ectasia.

Q3895
Refractive Surgery
In wavefront analysis, what type of optical aberration is coma?
Correct answer: A. third-order aberration
Rationale
Optical aberrations measured by wavefront analyzers, such as the Hartmann-Shack wavefront sensor, can be described by an infinite number of systems.  One set of descriptors are Zernike polynomials, which are the mathematical formulas used to describe surfaces.
Lower-order (second-order) aberrations include myopia, hyperopia, and regular astigmatism. Myopia produces positive defocus, hyperopia produces negative defocus, and regular (cylindrical) astigmatism produces a wavefront aberration that has orthogonal and oblique components.
The most important Zernike coefficients affecting visual quality are coma, trefoil, and spherical aberration. Coma and trefoil are third-order aberrations. With coma, light rays at one edge of the pupil come into focus before rays at the opposite edge; the effective image resembles a comet, having vertical and horizontal components. Coma is common in patients with decentered corneal grafts, keratoconus, and decentered laser ablations.
Spherical aberration, a fourth-order aberration, is the most clinically significant higher-order aberration. It increases depth of field, but decreases contrast sensitivity, causes night myopia, results in halos around point images, and is commonly increased after myopic laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) and surface ablation.

For a patient with a persistent epithelial defect following photorefractive keratectomy, what treatment would you recommend?
Correct answer: C. punctal occlusion
Rationale
A frequent cause of delayed re-epithelialization after PRK is dry eye. Hence, appropriate treatment for these patients includes lubrication with artificial tears or ointments, or cyclosporine drops, and/or punctal occlusion. A bandage contact lens or patching may also help. Topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and topical anesthetic drops can result in a corneal melting, especially in someone whose corneal epithelium is not intact. Therefore, they are not part of the treatment in this patient. Phototherapeutic keratectomy would not help with treatment of a persistent epithelial defect. Patients with diabetes, patients who smoke, or patients with an undiagnosed connective tissue disease may also have delayed healing.

Corneal transplantation may be required after refractive surgery.  What prior refractive surgery might require a larger transplant and additional sutures into the area of prior surgery?
Correct answer: A. radial keratotomy (RK)
Rationale
Corneal transplantation is occasionally required after refractive surgery for indications including significant scarring, irregular astigmatism, ectasia, and edema. Issues unrelated to refractive surgery, such as trauma, infectious keratitis, or corneal edema after cataract surgery, can also necessitate corneal transplantation. What is the most concerning complication of epithelial ingrowth after laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK)?
Correct answer: A. flap melt
Rationale
Epithelial ingrowth occurs more commonly in patients having LASIK when epithelial basement membrane dystrophy is present preoperatively, with flap trauma or with flap lift enhancements. The epithelium under the flap prevents nutrition of the flap, leading to melting of the flap.
Ectasia and DLK are not related to epithelial ingrowth. Infectious keratitis does not typically lead to or result from epithelial ingrowth.

Six months after refractive surgery, a patient has a visually significant central island. He wants additional laser treatment to treat this problem. What counselling should he be given about the treatment options for central islands?
Correct answer: C. With observation most central islands diminish over time, although resolution may take 6-12 months.
Rationale
A central island is defined as a steepening of at least 1.00 D with a diameter of less than 1 mm, compared with the paracentral flattened area. Central islands may be associated with decreased visual acuity, monocular diplopia and multiplopia, ghost images, and decreased contrast sensitivity. Central islands are becoming less common with use of variable spot size lasers. Most central islands diminish over time, especially after surface ablation, although resolution may take 6-12 months. The treatment outcomes are unpredictable no matter what approach is used; however, options such as topography-guided ablations may be helpful in treating persistent central islands. Haze formation is a risk of surface ablation.

Q4051
Refractive Surgery
In the Helmholtz theory, what happens to achieve accommodation?
Correct answer: C. Zonules relax.
Rationale
In the Helmholtz theory, the lens increases in dioptric power and “rounds up” at the anterior and posterior surfaces because when the ciliary muscles contract, all zonules relax. The lens diameter decreases and the pupil constricts during accommodation. In the Schachar theory of accommodation, ciliary contraction and an increase in equatorial zonular tension results in increased lens curvature. In that theory, presbyopia occurs because the increase in lens diameter reduces increases in equatorial zonular tension, and anything that increases resting equatorial zonular tension, such as scleral expansion, should restore accommodation.

Following manifest refraction, a patient has a best-corrected visual acuity of 20/40. She wants LASIK to improve her distance vision. Given her topography as shown, what counsel should the ophthalmologist give her?
Correct answer: C. She is not a candidate for ablative refractive surgery.
Rationale
This patient has corneal topography that shows inferior steepening that is most consistent with keratoconus (KCN). Topography is important in the preoperative evaluation of refractive surgery, as it may aid in the detection of early KCN.
