Q2923
Glaucoma
What constitutes the classic clinical triad of primary congenital glaucoma?
Correct answer: A. epiphora, photophobia, and blepharospasm
Rationale
Epiphora, photophobia, and blepharospasm constitute the classical clinical triad of primary congenital glaucoma. Other signs include clouding and enlargement of the cornea (first figure). Photophobia, epiphora, and blepharospasm result from the glare and epithelial abnormalities associated with corneal edema and opacification. Corneal edema results from elevated IOP and may be gradual or sudden in onset. Corneal edema is often the presenting sign in infants younger than 3 months. Microcystic edema initially involves the corneal epithelium but later extends to the stroma, often accompanied by one or more curvilinear breaks in Descemet membrane (Haab striae, second figure).
Although the corneal edema may resolve with IOP reduction, a scar will remain permanently at the site of the Haab striae. A number of other conditions may also cause corneal edema or Descemet tears (such as birth trauma). Corneal enlargement occurs with gradual stretching of the cornea as a result of elevated IOP. A corneal diameter of greater than 11.5 mm is suggestive of glaucoma in a newborn, while a diameter greater than 12.5 mm suggests glaucoma at the age of 1 year. Glaucoma should be suspected in any child with a corneal diameter greater than 13.0 mm. Blepharophimosis is not a clinical feature of primary congenital glaucoma.

Q2924
Glaucoma
What is the preferred first-line treatment for primary congenital glaucoma in a child with an edematous cornea?
Correct answer: D. trabeculotomy
Rationale
The primary treatment for childhood glaucoma is surgery. The first-line treatment for primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) is angle surgery, either goniotomy or trabeculotomy. Because goniotomy requires an incision to be made across the trabecular meshwork under direct gonioscopic visualization, a clear cornea is necessary to enable this surgery. In a child with PCG and an edematous cornea, trabeculotomy would be the preferred treatment. In trabeculotomy, an external approach is used to identify and cannulate the Schlemm canal and connect it with the anterior chamber through incision of the trabecular meshwork. A tube shunt or cyclophotocoagulation can be used to treat PCG but are used only if angle surgeries have failed.

Q2926
Glaucoma
A 2-day-old infant has tearing and photophobia. Examination under anesthesia reveals horizontal breaks in Descemet membrane. What is the most likely diagnosis?
Correct answer: B. congenital glaucoma
Rationale
Pertinent clinical features in congenital glaucoma include (1) corneal edema; (2) increased corneal diameter (greater than 12 mm); (3) elevated intraocular pressure; (4) Haab striae, which are tears in Descemet membrane oriented horizontally or concentric to the limbus.
Birth trauma, on the other hand, presents with corneal edema but with vertical or oblique posterior corneal striae. Peters anomaly presents with a central congenital corneal leukoma, iris strands adherent to leukoma, and variable involvement of the crystalline lens. Sclerocornea presents with noninflammatory scleralization of the cornea with an ill-defined limbus, and vascularization that can affect part of the cornea or the entire cornea. It is commonly associated with cornea plana and angle structure malformation.

What is the most common inheritance pattern in inherited cases of congenital glaucoma?
Correct answer: B. autosomal recessive
Rationale
Primary congenital glaucoma, also commonly called congenital or infantile glaucoma, usually occurs sporadically, but it can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. One gene, CYP1B1, on band 2p21, has been shown to cause primary congenital glaucoma.

Q2930
Glaucoma
What is the most likely cause of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) in a 18-month-old boy?
Correct answer: D. isolated trabeculodysgenesis
Rationale
Primary congenital glaucoma is characterized by isolated trabeculodysgenesis, which accounts for the majority of primary pediatric glaucomas. Most cases are diagnosed within the first year of life, and prognosis is best when glaucoma is diagnosed between 3 and 12 months of age.
Glaucoma associated with Sturge-Weber syndrome may occur in infancy or late childhood with the former being more likely. When glaucoma develops in infancy in this syndrome, the mechanism is isolated trabeculodysgenesis, whereas in late childhood or adolescence, the mechanism is elevated episcleral venous pressure due to the arteriovenous shunts in episcleral hemangiomas.
Pupillary block due to a microspherophakic lens is seen in Weill-Marchesani syndrome and usually presents in late childhood or young adulthood. High myopia with a shallow anterior chamber in a young adult should raise suspicion for this condition.
Aniridia is characterized by profound iris hypoplasia, which is present at birth. Glaucoma, however, may develop in late childhood or early adulthood, possibly due to postsurgical changes in patients who have had angle surgery.

Q2931
Glaucoma
What abnormally developed ocular structure is the pathologic cause of primary congenital glaucoma?
Correct answer: B. angle structures
Rationale
The basic pathophysiologic defect in primary congenital glaucoma is decreased aqueous outflow due to abnormal development of neural crest–derived tissue of the anterior chamber angle. Although abnormalities of the Descemet membrane, iris, and optic nerve all occur in primary congenital glaucoma, they are secondary effects from the increased intraocular pressure that occurs in this disease.

What is the inheritance pattern of juvenile open-angle glaucoma (JOAG)?
Correct answer: B. autosomal dominant
Rationale
Juvenile open-angle glaucoma (JOAG) is associated with the GLC1A myocilin gene (MYOC). It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Q2933

What ocular condition are curvilinear tears in Descemet membrane most often seen in?
Correct answer: C. primary congenital glaucoma
Rationale
Tears in Descemet membrane (Haab striae) are seen because of a rapid stretching of the cornea in primary congenital glaucoma. They are not seen in congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED) or Peters anomaly. Trauma from amniocentesis can result in a penetrating injury of the cornea. Tears in Descemet membrane can also be seen in a forceps injury sustained during delivery, but these are usually linear, not curvilinear.

A 3-year-old child has a history of primary infantile glaucoma. The child's condition remains poorly controlled despite maximal medical therapy and previous goniotomy on 2 separate occasions. What is the most appropriate next step in treatment?
Correct answer: B. glaucoma drainage device implant (GDI) surgery

Surgical intervention is the treatment of choice for primary congenital glaucoma presenting in infancy and early childhood. Q2935
Glaucoma
What are the gonioscopic findings in a patient with plateau iris configuration?
Correct answer: D. a narrow angle with a peripheral iris hump upon indentation
Rationale
Plateau iris configuration is caused by anterior rotated ciliary processes that hold up the peripheral iris. The anterior chamber is narrow peripherally but deep centrally, with a flat iris approach. On indentation, a peripheral hump is seen where the iris is draped over the anteriorly placed ciliary processes.
In a narrow angle caused by pupillary block, indentation would either open the angle or reveal peripheral synechiae.
An open angle can narrow on indentation if a large diameter Goldmann lens is used for gonioscopy.

Q2936
Glaucoma
What is the desired observed treatment effect during endocyclophotocoagulation (ECP)?
Correct answer: D. ciliary process shrinkage
Rationale
Endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation is performed through a clear corneal or limbal incision with direct visualization of the ciliary processes. Power is titrated to achieve shrinking and whitening of the ciliary processes. Bubble formation and ciliary process rupture should be avoided to minimize postoperative inflammation. Photocoagulation lightens rather than darkens the tissue.

What corneal complication is most likely with the use of antifibrotic agents for trabeculectomy?
Correct answer: A. epithelial defects
Rationale
Antifibrotic agents, especially 5-fluorouracil, are toxic to the corneal epithelium and can cause persistent corneal epithelial defects. Endothelial damage can occur with the use of antifibrotics but is not common with the current surgical technique and dosage. Keratic precipitates and band keratopathy are not associated with the use of antifibrotic agents.

Q2939
Glaucoma
What condition is associated with elevated episcleral venous pressure?
Correct answer: C. carotid-cavernous sinus fistula
Rationale
Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener granulomatosis) is a chronic systemic disorder involving vasculitis of small- and medium-size vessels in many organs but most commonly of the upper respiratory tract and the kidneys. There is no known association with elevated episcleral venous pressure (EVP).
Angle recession occurs when the ciliary body is torn, usually between the longitudinal and circular fibers. The typical gonioscopic appearance consists of torn iris processes, visible scleral spur, and an abnormally widened ciliary body. Though there may acutely be microhyphema, there is no EVP or reflux of blood into Schlemm canal.
Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome is characterized by a prominent anteriorly displaced Schwalbe line, iris hypoplasia, corectopia, and dental anomalies. There is no association with EVP.
Elevated episcleral venous pressure (EVP) can be caused by venous obstruction (as in thyroid ophthalmopathy or retrobulbar tumor) or arteriovenous anomalies (as in carotid-cavernous sinus fistula or Sturge-Weber syndrome).

Q2941
Glaucoma
How is uveoscleral outflow affected by age, pressure, and cycloplegia?
Correct answer: C. pressure-insensitive, decreases with age, increased by cycloplegic agents
Rationale
Uveoscleral outflow is the nontrabecular outflow of the eye. It is pressure-insensitive and the predominant mechanism is one of aqueous passage from the anterior chamber into the ciliary muscle and then to the supraciliary and suprachoroidal spaces, exiting the eye through an intact sclera or along the nerves and the vessels that penetrate it. It can account for up to 45% of the total aqueous outflow. Uveoscleral outflow decreases with age and in patients with glaucoma. It is increased by prostaglandin analogues, and cycloplegic and adrenergic agents and cyclodialysis clefts early on. The opposite effect is seen with miotics. Outflow for the uveoscleral pathway cannot be measured and is therefore calculated by the Goldmann equation.

Q2944
Glaucoma
What pattern of visual field defect is most typical of glaucoma?
Correct answer: D. arcuate scotoma
Rationale
Patterns of visual field loss in glaucoma correspond to the retinal nerve fiber layer anatomy. Glaucomatous visual field defects include arcuate scotoma, paracentral scotoma, nasal step, altitudinal defect, temporal wedge, and generalized depression. An arcuate scotoma is an arc-shaped defect that occurs 10 to 20 degrees from fixation; a complete arcuate defect arches from the blind spot to the nasal raphe and is wider and closer to fixation on the nasal side. This defect is a result of nerve fiber bundle damage at the superotemporal or inferotemporal disc, common areas of damage in glaucomatous optic neuropathy.
A centrocecal scotoma involves both the blind spot and the point of fixation and is seen in conditions such as toxic optic neuropathy, nutritional optic neuropathy, and Leber hereditary optic neuropathy. Hemianopic defects are caused by retrochiasmal disease, and blind spot enlargement is more often seen in papilledema and chorioretinal inflammatory conditions such as multiple evanescent white dot syndrome and acute idiopathic blind spot enlargement.

Q2946
Glaucoma
What instrument is most accurate when measuring intraocular pressure (IOP) in the setting of corneal scarring?
Correct answer: B. Tono-Pen tonometer
Rationale
In the setting of corneal scarring, devices that can applanate a small area of unaffected cornea, such as the Tono-Pen or pneumotonometer, are more accurate. Although the Goldmann tonometer is considered the standard for IOP measurement, its accuracy and reliability are reduced in the setting of corneal edema and corneal scarring. The Perkins tonometer is a portable version of the Goldmann instrument and has the same limitations. A noncontact tonometer uses an air puff to distort the cornea. The reflection from the air puff may be asymmetrical and distorted in the setting of corneal scarring, resulting in variations in readings for different areas of the cornea.

Q2947
Glaucoma
What mechanism of angle-closure glaucoma is effectively treated with an iridotomy?
Correct answer: A. pupillary block
Rationale
An iridotomy specifically addresses pupillary block. It allows equalization of the pressure between the anterior and posterior chambers in cases where aqueous cannot easily flow through the pupil. This, in turn, relieves any iris bombé that can lead to angle closure. An iridotomy is not useful in cases of synechial angle closure, including neovascular glaucoma and iridocorneal endothelial syndrome. It also does not relieve any posterior pushing mechanisms of angle closure, such as aqueous misdirection, suprachoroidal hemorrhage, or anterior choroidal effusions.

Q2948
Glaucoma
What region, as indicated by the arrows, consists of fenestrated connective tissue providing structural support for the optic nerve?"
Correct answer: D. the laminar region
Rationale
The lamina cribrosa lies in the laminar region of the anterior optic nerve. It consists of fenestrated connective tissue lamellae and provides the main structural support for the optic nerve as it exits the eye. It is made primarily of collagen; other extracellular matrix components include elastin, laminin and fibronectin. Capillary beams nourish this critical region as the neural components of the optic nerve pass through the connective tissue beams. Two central fenestrations allow transit of the central retinal artery and vein.
The connective tissue density within the lamina has been described histologically as lesser superiorly and inferiorly, as compared with the temporal and nasal aspects of the optic nerve. It has been suggested that these differences play a role in the development of glaucomatous optic neuropathy. The pores of the lamina cribrosa can be seen on ophthalmoscopy at the base of the optic nerve head cup.
The nerve fiber layer of the retina is the most superficial and anterior zone of the anterior optic nerve, composed of the axons of the retinal ganglion cells, visible with a red-free (green) filter. The next posterior layer is the prelaminar region, which lies adjacent to the peripapillary choroid. The laminar region is then continuous with the sclera and composed of the lamina cribrosa. The retrolaminar region is the most posterior segment of the anterior optic nerve and found immediately posterior to the lamina cribrosa. It is marked by the beginning of axonal myelination and is surrounded by leptomeninges of the central nervous system.

What initial therapy should be avoided in a 35-year-old patient with moderate glaucoma and a history of asthma?
Correct answer: A. nonselective beta blocker (eg, timolol)
Rationale
Prostaglandin analogs and topical beta blockers are often used as initial therapy in glaucoma patients because they can achieve a 20% to 30% intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction with once-daily dosing. Q2958
Glaucoma
What is the most reliable way to assess a narrow angle?
Correct answer: B. gonioscopy
Rationale
Gonioscopy continues to be the gold standard of angle evaluation and provides more information than current imaging techniques. While ultrasound biomicroscopy and anterior segment OCT can provide valuable information, the particular advantage of gonioscopy lies in the ability to indent, allowing one to assess whether peripheral anterior synechiae have formed in the setting of a narrow angle. This is particularly important because the management of appositional versus synechial angle closure is different; a peripheral iridotomy is not indicated if the angle is synechially closed 360°. The van Herick method is a quick way of assessing angle depth, but does not give any direct information about angle anatomy.

Q2960
Glaucoma
By what mechanism do topical beta blockers reduce intraocular pressure?
Correct answer: D. reducing aqueous production by the ciliary body epithelium
Rationale
Topical medications for the treatment of glaucoma can reduce the formation of aqueous and/or improve outflow of aqueous. Topical beta blockers reduce aqueous production by the ciliary epithelium by inhibiting cAMP production. This reduces aqueous secretion by 20% to 50%. Alpha agonists also reduce cAMP production via binding to the alpha 2 adrenoceptors.
With long-term use, brimonidine has been shown to also increase uveoscleral outflow. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors inhibit ciliary epithelium carbonic anhydrase, which reduces aqueous production. Parasympathomimetics cause contraction of the longitudinal ciliary muscle fibers, which in turn increases outflow through the trabecular meshwork by causing tension on the scleral spur. Prostaglandin analogues increase uveoscleral outflow through an unknown mechanism, though it is thought to be through activation of matrix metalloproteinases, which in turn remodels the tissues along the uveoscleral pathway.

Three months after a vitreous hemorrhage, an intraocular pressure of 35 mm Hg is measured. The angle is open and abundant tan-colored cells are observed in the anterior chamber with a trace amount of flare. What are these cells most likely to be?
Correct answer: D. ghost cells
Rationale
Ghost-cell glaucoma occurs in association with vitreous hemorrhage. Red blood cells (RBCs) degenerate in the vitreous, migrate forward to the anterior chamber (AC) through a disrupted anterior hyaloid face (usually due to vitrectomy, cataract surgery, or trauma), and then obstruct the trabecular meshwork. The degenerated RBCs appear as tan-colored spheres known as ghost cells, which contain clumps of denatured hemoglobin. Hemolytic glaucoma is similar to ghost cell glaucoma except that in hemolytic glaucoma, macrophages phagocytize RBC debris and then occlude the trabecular meshwork. The macrophages appear as reddish cells in the aqueous and the trabecular meshwork has a reddish-brown discoloration.

SLT delivers less energy than ALT. SLT is based on the principle of selective photothermolysis targeting pigmented cells. Correct answer: A. iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (ICE)
Rationale
Iridocorneal endothelial syndrome is characterized by abnormal corneal endothelium that has epithelium-like characteristics and extends from the peripheral cornea onto the trabecular meshwork and iris. It causes a secondary angle-closure glaucoma. There are 3 variants (essential [progressive] iris atrophy, Chandler syndrome, and Cogan-Reese syndrome), with varying amounts of corneal and iris changes. The Chandler variant of ICE syndrome is characterized by corneal edema. ICE syndrome is almost always unilateral and is more common in women.
Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome is characterized by iris changes similar to those in ICE syndrome, and 50% of persons with this syndrome develop glaucoma. However, unlike ICE syndrome, the disease is bilateral and the peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) insert into a prominent Schwalbe line with no extension beyond it.
Posterior polymorphous corneal dystrophy is characterized by bilateral endothelial changes including vesicles, bands, and geographic opacities. Many affected individuals are asymptomatic. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and iris abnormalities (corectopia and PAS) can occur in this condition but are not common.
Fuchs dystrophy is an endothelial dystrophy with findings ranging from asymptomatic central corneal guttae to cornea edema and painful bullous keratopathy. It is more common in women with onset after 50 years of age and is not associated with glaucoma.

Correct answer: C. scleral buckle

Q3210
Glaucoma
Once the aqueous humor leaves the anterior chamber, what is the order of intraocular structures through which it passes in the pressure-sensitive outflow pathway?
Correct answer: D. uveal trabecular meshwork, corneoscleral trabecular meshwork, juxtacanalicular meshwork, Schlemm canal, outlet or collector channels, episcleral veins
Rationale
There are 2 major paths the aqueous takes to exit the eye that include the pressure-sensitive trabecular outflow and the pressure-insensitive uveoscleral outflow. To recall which is which, remember that "sensitive" and "trabecular" start with consonants and "insensitive" and "uveoscleral" start with vowels.
The pathway of aqueous through the pressure-sensitive trabecular outflow starts with the trabecular meshwork. The classically described layers of the trabecular meshwork are uveal (adjacent to anterior chamber, bands extending from the iris root/ciliary body to the peripheral cornea), corneoscleral (sheets of trabeculum from scleral spur to lateral wall of scleral sulcus), and juxtacanalicular (most outflow resistance, adjacent to/forms the inner wall of Schlemm canal). Of note, the trabecular cells are phagocytic, which is particularly relevant in the setting of inflammation and laser trabeculoplasty. (Mnemonic: Undercover job = uveal, corneoscleral, juxtacanalicular)
Next, the aqueous passes through Schlemm canal which resembles a lymphatic vessel. It is a single channel (200-300 μm) lined by an endothelial layer with a discontinuous basement membrane.
From Schlemm canal, approximately 25 to 30 collector channels arise and drain into the deep and midscleral venous plexi. Though not clearly delineated in the BCSC Glaucoma section, this is covered in the BCSC Fundamentals and Principles of Ophthalmology section.
Eventually, via a complex system of vessels, the aqueous reaches the episcleral veins and subsequently flows into the anterior ciliary and superior ophthalmic veins, which drain into the cavernous sinus.
The pressure-insensitive uveoscleral outflow likely has many paths; however, the answer choice "ciliary muscle, suprachoroidal space, scleral wall" describes the predominant one. The aqueous passes from the anterior chamber into the ciliary muscle, then into supraciliary and suprachoroidal spaces and finally through intact sclera or along the nerves and vessels penetrating it. The uveoscleral pathway may account for up to 45% of total aqueous outflow. It is increased by cycloplegia, adrenergic agents, and prostaglandin analogues. It is decreased with age, glaucoma, and by miotics.
One of the remaining incorrect answers has the incorrect order, and the other is a mix of structures from the pressure-sensitive and pressure-insensitive pathways.

A patient develops uncontrolled glaucoma several months after an extracapsular cataract extraction. A grayish, sheetlike opacity is noted on the superior corneal endothelial surface. What test will confirm the diagnosis?
Correct answer: C. argon laser
Rationale
Argon laser can confirm the diagnosis of epithelial ingrowth by producing white burns on the epithelial membrane and charcoal burns on normal iris surface. Extent of the ingrowth can also be demarcated by this method.
Anterior chamber paracentesis (tap) is not indicated; it can define an infectious reaction rather than an inflammatory reaction with rise in intraocular pressure.
Endothelial cell count may show a decreased cell number; however, specular microscopy would be a better diagnostic tool to define epithelial ingrowth, reversing the appearance of epithelial cells with dark centers and a lighter periphery.
Gonioscopy to reveal retained lens fragments in the angle would not be likely to produce a finding of epithelial downgrowth, although it may reveal an epithelial sheet in the angle. The majority of cases of glaucoma caused by epithelial downgrowth have normal gonioscopic findings.

Q3212
Glaucoma
During the creation of a peripheral iridotomy, the 10 o'clock and 2 o'clock locations are avoided to decrease the risk of what complication?
Correct answer: A. dysphotopsias
Rationale
Complications from iridotomy include bleeding, persistent postoperative inflammation, accelerated cataract formation, and lens damage. Retinal tear, postoperative IOP spike, and corneal endothelial damage can also occur.
Visual dysphotopsias (glare, streaks, lines, halos) may be present and are thought to result from proximity of the iridotomy to the tear meniscus, which acts as a strong prism. Thus, some clinicians advocate temporal placement of the iridotomy, taking precautions to perform the iridotomy at a distance from the tear meniscus. However, it is unclear whether the location of the iridotomy affects the likelihood of dysphotopsias, and a large prospective study suggests that it does not.

What laser settings are best for performing a laser peripheral iridoplasty?
Correct answer: C. low power, long duration, large spot size
Rationale
Laser iridoplasty is often used in plateau iris or other conditions with residual angle closure after an iridotomy. The goal is to create iris contraction peripherally, pulling the tissue from the angle. This requires a low and slow burn, using a large spot size. High powers ablate instead of shrink the tissue. Short durations do not allow enough time for adequate tissue shrinkage.

Q3214
Glaucoma
A 44-year-old hyperopic Chinese woman is noted on slit-lamp examination to have shallow anterior chambers and clear lenses. Gonioscopy shows a convex iris profile with 360 degrees of iridotrabecular contact OU. On indentation gonioscopy, the scleral spur is visible for 360 degrees OU. Applanation IOP is 13 mm Hg OU and the optic nerves have healthy rims with a C:D ratio of 0.3 OU. What is the most likely mechanism of angle closure?
Correct answer: A. pupillary block
Rationale
This patient has bilateral appositional angle closure without IOP elevation or optic nerve damage and can be categorized as a primary angle-closure suspect. Primary angle closure is multifactorial, and pupillary block is the most common mechanism. Pupillary block occurs when there is restricted movement through the pupil because of iris contact with the lens and is maximal when the pupil is in a mid-dilated position. The restricted flow of aqueous causes a pressure differential between the posterior and anterior chambers, resulting in forward bowing of the peripheral iris. The treatment for pupillary block angle closure is an iridotomy.
In plateau iris configuration, the anterior chamber is relatively deep, and the iris plane appears flat. Angle closure is caused by anteriorly positioned ciliary processes that push the peripheral iris forward.
Ciliary block is a rare form of secondary angle closure that usually presents following surgery in patients with primary angle closure. It presents with uniform shallowing or flattening of both the central and peripheral anterior chamber, with anterior displacement of the iris-lens diaphragm. In this patient with bilateral angle closure without any previous history of laser or surgery, ciliary block is extremely unlikely.
In phacomorphic angle closure, the predominant mechanism of angle closure is the mass effect of the lens. The lens is usually cataractous and causes a slow and progressive narrowing of the angle due to its bulk. In some cases, the onset may be rapid due to marked lens swelling (intumescent lens). In this patient with clear lenses, a phacomorphic mechanism is not likely to be the primary cause of angle closure.
